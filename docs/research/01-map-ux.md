# Knowledge map UX: heatmap vs graph vs hybrid

Build a hybrid: a 5-row topic x concept mastery grid as the overview, with graph structure shown only locally and on demand (cells ordered by prerequisite depth; tapping a cell opens a short prerequisite strip and the gap-session button). The research supports this split: simple inspectable displays with colour-coding are the most used and preferred open learner models, a Mastery-Grids-style grid has classroom evidence of engagement, and in the one study that compared 17 views students rated a prerequisite map highest for deciding what to work on next while a free-form network and treemaps rated lowest. Shipped products point the same way: Khan Academy removed its node-link knowledge map, Duolingo replaced its tree with a guided path, and Math Academy and Execute Program keep the graph in the engine and show a simple fill-in display. For our graph the grid loses little: 35 of 38 prerequisite edges sit inside a topic, so depth-ordered rows carry most of the structure, whereas a whole-graph view cannot fit 40 tappable, labelled nodes on a phone without pan/zoom or scrolling. The evidence is thin where it matters most for us: no study compares grid and graph on real learning or real choices, none uses working developers on phones, and nothing speaks to a 3-minute demo, so "visibly fills in" has to be met by design (animate each state change when it happens) and checked with testers.

## Recommendations

- **must**: Ship the hybrid for the demo: a mastery grid as the overview (5 topic rows, 8 concept cells per row, cells ordered left to right by prerequisite depth), with graph structure shown only for the selected concept. Do not build a whole-graph node-link screen for the demo. Keep the screen name 'Knowledge map'. _Why:_ The grid shows all 40 concepts at tappable size on one screen with stable positions, which the 'visibly fills in' step needs; a full graph needs pan/zoom or scrolling on a phone (20 nodes at depth 1). Depth ordering keeps 35 of 38 prerequisite edges readable as 'left comes first'. Simple colour-coded views are the preferred OLM form, Khan removed its node-link map, and the prerequisite view that students rated best is delivered locally (next item).
- **must**: Tapping a cell opens a bottom sheet with: the concept title, its state in words, a prerequisite strip (unlearned prerequisites in topological order, then the concept, then what it unlocks, each chip in its state colour), and one primary button such as 'Start gap session: 2 reels + 1 check'. For locked cells the sheet says which prerequisite is missing. _Why:_ A prerequisite layout scored highest for 'what to work on next' (4.4 of 5) and the local graph is the part of graph views people report using. In Mastery Grids only 48% quickly learned how to get from the grid to content and about a third used it for access, so the action must be an explicit button, not an implied tap. The strip is exactly the chain the gap-session spec already computes.
- **must**: Encode the five ordered levels (unseen, seen, checked, learned, retained) as one single-hue ramp plus a redundant shape cue (for example empty outline, small dot, half fill, full fill, full fill with a tick). Encode the two flags on separate channels: due as an amber ring or dot, locked as a dashed outline with a lock glyph. Use amber for the Review pool chip in the feed as well. _Why:_ Ordered levels read best as one ramp (Mastery Grids used colour saturation; Khan uses five stepped levels), and both Apple HIG and WCAG 1.4.1 say not to rely on colour alone. Putting due and locked on other channels lets a cell be 'learned and due' without a seventh colour. Matching amber between the feed's review chip and the map's due marker links 'an earlier concept resurfaces' to the map. The specific palette is a design judgment, not an evidence-backed finding.
- **must**: Show each state change at the moment it happens, in the feed: after a card is answered, show a small 5x8 dot version of the map for about 1.5 seconds with the affected cell animating to its new level and a line of text ('Checked to Learned'). On the dashboard, mark cells that changed since the last visit. _Why:_ In 3 minutes a learner sees about 5 reels and 2 cards, so roughly 4 cells become seen, 1-2 checked and 1 learned out of 40. Without animation at the moment of change the map will look almost empty when opened. Khan shows a 'leveled up / leveled down' summary after every activity. No research covers a 3-minute demo, so this is a design inference to verify with the demo script.
- **should**: Put a one-line summary and a per-topic stacked bar above the grid ('Learned 1 of 40, Checked 2, Seen 4, Due 1'), and count only box 2 or higher as learned in the headline number. _Why:_ Skill meters are the most common and best-rated simple OLM view (4.0-4.1) and give the at-a-glance total the grid lacks. Khan's headline percentage counts only Proficient and Mastered, the same rule as 'learned needs a delayed correct answer', and it keeps the map honest about watch-only progress.
- **should**: Keep 'retained' visibly above 'learned' and keep the due marker on learned cells, so the map never looks finished after one delayed check. _Why:_ The documented criticism of Execute Program is that its display tops out at 'Learned' after one review and users then feel done. Math Academy's lead wants to show decay for the same reason. In the demo the due marker is also what makes the review step visible on the map.
- **should**: Surface two or three suggested gaps explicitly (a 'Suggested next' row or a pulsing outline): due cells first, then unlocked-but-not-learned cells with the most dependents. Do not rely on learners reading the colours to find gaps. _Why:_ Only 29% of Mastery Grids users agreed the grid helped them identify weak points or plan, even though most liked it. The demo step 'tap a gap' is faster and more reliable if one cell is clearly marked as the gap to tap.
- **should**: When a cell is selected, show its relations in place: highlight its prerequisites and dependents, outline RELATED_TO neighbours differently, dim the rest, and draw connector lines only for that one concept. _Why:_ This gives the path-following benefit of node-link diagrams (the one task where they consistently win) without drawing all 60 edges, and it is the only place the 18 cross-topic RELATED_TO edges become visible. react-native-svg and Reanimated are both listed as 'Included in Expo Go' in the SDK 57 docs, so no dev build is needed.
- **could**: Log map use and treat the grid-versus-graph question as open after the demo: add events for map opened and cell tapped (concept, state), keep the source on gap_session_started, and if a graph view is wanted later, prototype a per-topic 8-node prerequisite drill-down (it fits at 44 pt) and compare it with the grid on a 'pick what to work on next' task with testers. _Why:_ The comparative evidence is one perception study with 33 students and screenshots; nothing measures real choices or learning, and nothing involves working developers on phones. Tester logs are the cheapest way to find out whether the map is used at all and whether a graph view would add anything.

## Fact-check: changes to the recommendations

- State encoding (rec 3): 'locked' cannot be an overlay flag with a lock glyph. On a fresh profile 34 of 40 cells are locked and only the 6 roots are available, so the map would open as a wall of locks. Make locked the quiet default (dim, no glyph), make 'available now' the visually marked state, and reserve the lock explanation for the bottom sheet.
- Make the unlock the main 'map fills in' moment (recs 4 and 7): the first learned root unlocks 2-6 cells at once (rn-core-components unlocks the 6 other React Native cells; the other roots unlock 2-3). Animate that wave in the post-card mini-map and on the dashboard, and steer the demo script (onboarding pick, Focus mode) so the React Native root is the one that gets the delayed check. One cell changing shade out of 40 will not read as 'filling in'.
- Do not design the demo around 'retained' or 'learned and due' (recs 3, 5, 6): with the demo clock (60 s, then 3 min, then 6 min) box 3 needs more than 4 minutes after the first check, and a learned cell is not due for 3 minutes. Keep the levels in the model and legend, but for the 3-minute script either accept that the ramp shows only seen / checked / learned, or ship a seeded 'returning learner' profile to show the full ramp. The due marker will also rarely be visible on the map, because the overdue guard serves a due review in the next slot (Focus) or within 2 slots (Light); show the review link in the feed chip instead of relying on the map marker.
- Due marker and ramp colours (rec 3): do not put an amber ring directly on a sky fill (1.0:1 luminance contrast). Use a badge with a dark separator or a distinct shape outside the fill. Treat the shape cue as required, not redundant: the low steps of a single-hue sky ramp are 1.4-2.4:1 against #0B1220 and adjacent steps differ by only 1.3-1.9:1. Three clearly separated fill steps plus outline and tick is safer than five shades.
- Headline rule (rec 5): keep 'only box >= 2 counts as learned' because it is the plan's own definition, but drop the Khan justification. Khan's percentage counts Proficient, which needs no delay (100% on one exercise), and that calculation is new; Khan's reimagined classroom view drops the course percentage for 'X of Y skills to Proficient' per unit. The usable precedent is a per-topic 'X of 8 learned' counter, which also fits the grid rows better than a global 'Learned 1 of 40'.
- Bottom sheet button (rec 2): compute the label, do not hard-code '2 reels + 1 check'. Per PLAN.md a gap session spends 3 items per target (intro reel, one intervening item, card) and ends after 6 items. That label is true only for cells with no unlearned prerequisites (the 6 roots on a fresh profile). 19 cells have one ancestor (6 items, exactly the cap) and 15 cells have two or more, so the session cannot reach the tapped concept. For those, the sheet should offer 'Start with <first unmet prerequisite>' and say how many steps remain. Also a single correct check in a gap session only reaches box 1, so the tapped cell stays locked by the map's own rule; the sheet must say so or the plan must define an exception.
- Soften 'do not build a graph' (recs 1 and 9): the only comparative study rates a node-link prerequisite map above a grid for 'what to work on next' (4.4 vs 3.8), Math Academy does show learners a graph, and Ghoniem's matrix result does not apply to a mastery grid. Keep the grid as the overview for the demo, but promote the per-topic prerequisite mini-graph from 'could, after the demo' to the drill-down itself: 8 nodes, at most 6 per layer (6 x 44 pt + 5 x 8 pt = 304 pt, fits a 360 dp screen), shown when a row or cell is opened. This replaces the linear 'strip', which is trivial for 19 cells (root then concept) and cannot show the star and branch shapes the topics actually have.
- Grid layout (rec 1): 'ordered left to right by depth' carries less than claimed. Group cells by depth with visible gaps instead of 8 equal columns, because levels per topic differ (3, 4, 2, 4, 5), two rows have 6 cells tied at depth 1, and columns will not line up across rows. Also solve labels: 40 unlabelled cells force a tap per cell to learn what a gap is (Mastery Grids had labelled columns; Tondello's first lesson is clear descriptions). Options: a title readout line that follows touch/drag across cells, or 4 x 2 cells per topic (about 80 x 44 pt on a 375 pt screen) with two-line labels, which also meets the 44 pt / 48 dp target sizes that 8-per-row (37.5-43 pt, 4 pt gaps) misses.
- Suggested-next rule (rec 7): on a fresh profile 'unlocked but not learned with the most dependents' always resolves to the roots, with rn-core-components (6 dependents) first. That is fine for the demo, but say so in the script, and add 'just unlocked' as the first tier after due cells once anything is learned.
- Rec 8 rationale: keep 'show relations only for the selected concept', but cite it as a screen-size and clutter decision. The node-link versus matrix literature compares adjacency matrices and says a 40-node, 60-edge graph is readable; it does not rank a mastery grid.

## Fact-check: what the track missed

- Fresh-profile state of the map: per PLAN.md only learned concepts unlock dependents and every feed pool draws from unlocked concepts, so for the first 3 minutes the feed can only serve the 6 root concepts (18 reels) and the map is 6 available cells plus 34 locked. The track designed the encoding for a half-filled map that the demo never reaches.
- Demo timing against the plan's own tables: 'retained' is unreachable in 3 minutes; the loop closes only in Focus mode (first check at about 1:00-1:10, delayed review at about 2:10-2:50); in Light mode the first card comes after 4 reels (about 2:00) and the review after 3:00. The knowledge-map step therefore has roughly 10-40 seconds of the demo, which argues for one unmistakable change (the unlock wave) over subtle shading.
- Gap session versus the map: the 6-item cap and the 'learned = delayed correct answer' rule mean tapping most cells cannot deliver the tapped concept in one session, and a 6-item session is about 2-3 minutes, so it is outside the 3-minute loop. The demo script should end at 'map fills in' and show the gap tap only as an entry point (sheet opens, button visible).
- PLAN.md drift the map design must reconcile: M6 lists heatmap states as unseen / seen / checked / learned / retained plus a due marker, with no 'locked' state; the event union has gap_session_started but no map_opened or map_cell_tapped; the M6 acceptance test only asserts 'heatmap level becomes learned'. Adding locked/available states, the unlock animation and map events needs plan and event-schema changes.
- Palette contrast was computable and was left as an open question: text 17.5:1, muted 7.7:1, sky 11.2:1, amber 11.2:1, pink 7.1:1 on #0B1220 (9.6-14.9:1 on #13213D). Sky and amber are indistinguishable in luminance, and low-alpha sky fills fall below 3:1. Since re-rendering reels is cheap, the design-system track can pick a due colour that differs from sky in lightness as well as hue.
- Platform coverage: the plan sends an Android APK to testers first. Android's recommended touch target is 48 dp and common widths start at 360 dp, where 8 cells per row are 37.5 dp. The track sized cells against iPhone widths only, and used a dated 375-393 pt range (current iPhones are 390-440 pt).
- Screen-reader and non-visual access for a 40-cell grid: each cell needs an accessibility label with concept title, state in words and due/locked status, and the unlock/level-up animation needs a text equivalent (the 'Checked to Learned' line covers part of it). Not addressed.
- Khan Academy's newer 'X of Y skills to Proficient' per-unit counter and the reported plan to bring back a concept map are both more recent signals than the removal the track leaned on. Shipped-product evidence is mixed: Khan and Duolingo dropped whole-graph navigation, Math Academy ships a learner-facing graph and wants more of it.
- The track's evidence quality label: three of its 'high confidence' product claims rest on one staff forum reply, one podcast transcript and one undated personal note. They are accurate quotes but thin grounds for 'do not build a graph view'; the decision is better justified by our own constraints (phone width, 3-minute demo, 34 locked cells, engineering time) and should be presented that way.
- Method note for the caller: Khan Academy Help Center and Execute Program pages return 403 or empty to WebFetch; I read them in the Browser pane (read-only). No files were created or changed in the repo. Files consulted: docs/PLAN.md and content/software-engineering/graph.json.

## Findings

### 1. Across 64 open learner model (OLM) studies in higher education, simple inspectable models are preferred over advanced ones, and colour-coding is one of the two most-used features.

Hooshyar et al. 2020 systematic review (Computers & Education 154), abstract: OLMs 'have been mainly used to support learners' cognition and a bit less metacognition and motivation'; 'Although learners can edit or negotiate with their learning model in advanced ways, a simple inspectable OLM is more preferred'; 'Comparison and color-coding are two most-used features in OLMs'. Only the abstract was readable (publisher page blocked), so details of the 64 studies were not checked.

Source: <https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1016/j.compedu.2020.103878?fields=title,abstract,tldr,year,venue,openAccessPdf> · confidence: medium

### 2. In the only study found that compares many OLM views side by side, a prerequisite map was rated best for 'identify what to work on next', a grid was mid-table, and a free-form network and treemaps were near the bottom.

Bull, Brusilovsky, Guerra, Araujo (UMAP 2016 late-breaking), 33 students rating screenshots of 17 individual views on a 1-5 scale: Pre-requisites 4.4 (median 5), Hierarchical Tree 4.1 (median 5), Skill Meters 4.1/4.0, Graph 4.1, Bullets 3.9, Grid (Mastery Grids) 3.8, Concept Map 3.8, Network 3.4 (median 3), Treemaps 2.7/3.3, Word Cloud 3.1. Authors: structure alone does not explain it, since Concept Map and Network are also structured; 'it is the nature of the relationships and/or familiarity of the layout that makes the difference'. Limits they state: perceptions from screenshots of different systems and domains, not behaviour or learning.

Source: <https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-1618/LBR4.pdf> · confidence: high

### 3. A topic grid with colour intensity for mastery (Mastery Grids) engaged students and was liked, but most did not use it to pick weak points or to launch content.

Loboda, Guerra, Hosseini, Brusilovsky (EC-TEL 2014): grid columns are topics, rows are resource types, 'the more saturated the color the more mastery'; clicking a cell opens its activities. 26 of 31 active Java students used it; users answered more questions with a higher correct ratio (no significance test, small n). Survey (n=25): 70.8% found it useful, 76% said the 'Me' grid was useful at topic level, but only 29% agreed it helped identify weak points and 29% that it helped plan; 'only 48% ... quickly learned how to go from topics to activities and back', and only about a third used it for content access. A later fine-grained (topic plus concept) version 'considerably impacts student choice of learning activities' (Barria-Pineda et al. 2018, abstract only).

Source: <https://people.cs.pitt.edu/~hosseini/papers/ectel2014.pdf> · confidence: high

### 4. OLMs have measured benefits for learning and self-assessment accuracy, but the evidence comes from skill-meter style displays with school and university students; nothing found tests grid versus graph on learning outcomes, or adult professionals on phones.

Bull 2016 (open access): 'The most common early visualisation, which is still used today, was skill meters'; OLMs 'can result in significant learning gains and increased self-assessment accuracy (e.g. Brusilovsky et al. 2015; Kerly and Bull 2008; Long and Aleven 2013; Mitrovic and Martin 2007)'; node-link map views 'are considered helpful for identifying relationships' but 'choice of learner model visualisation may depend on learner preferences'; the cited evaluations are small (n=8 to n=110) and Bull calls most 'quite limited'. Long and Aleven's two classroom experiments (skill bars plus self-assessment prompts) are summarised as showing an OLM can enhance domain learning (Semantic Scholar summary; full text not opened).

Source: <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6302918/> · confidence: high

### 5. At 40 nodes and 60 edges a node-link diagram is within the range people can read; the hairball limit is higher. Node-link wins mainly on path-following tasks, tables and matrices on lookup tasks.

Yoghourdjian et al. 2020 (controlled study, 25-175 nodes): 'people have significant difficulty finding the shortest path in high-density node-link diagrams with more than 50 nodes and even low-density graphs with more than 100 nodes'. Ghoniem, Fekete, Castagliola (InfoVis 2004) abstract as quoted in search results: above twenty vertices matrices beat node-link on most tasks and 'only path finding is consistently in favor of node-link diagrams'; I could not open that full text (publisher blocks), so treat it as medium. Both are desktop studies; neither covers phones.

Source: <https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.07944> · confidence: medium

### 6. Our graph is shallow and wide, so a depth-ordered grid keeps most of the structure, while a whole-graph view cannot show 40 tappable nodes on one phone screen.

Computed from content/software-engineering/graph.json: 40 concepts, 38 PREREQUISITE_OF edges (35 within a topic, 3 cross-topic), 22 RELATED_TO edges (18 cross-topic), depth 0-4 with 6/20/7/5/2 concepts per level, max in-degree 2, max out-degree 6, titles average 17 characters (max 36). Apple HIG: iOS default control size 44x44 pt, minimum 28x28 pt, about 12 pt padding. The depth-1 layer has 20 nodes: 560 pt wide even at the 28 pt minimum, against a 375-393 pt screen, so a layered full graph needs pan/zoom; topic swimlanes need roughly 1.5 screens of scroll. Eight cells per row give about 38-42 pt cells on the same screens, and all 5 rows fit in about 360 pt of height. Titles do not fit inside cells in either design.

Source: <https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility> · confidence: high

### 7. Khan Academy shipped a node-link knowledge map and removed it; staff say linear progression worked better for most users, while some teachers still miss the map for showing how skills connect.

Khan Academy staff reply (Evan Lewis) in the Help Center community: 'This feature was removed after we introduced the updated Mission and then Course Mastery systems. Most students and teachers have found that the linear course progression works best for them, but I understand how this sort of dependency tree could be helpful in some instances'; of the old map: 'it loads very slowly, and the organization isn't the greatest'. Teachers in the same thread: 'I want to be able to show my students how the skills they are learning are related'. The map's builder wrote in 2010 they had 'absolutely zero proof that we've accomplished (or failed at) any of this' (bjk5.com/post/1664635835/constellation-knowledge).

Source: <https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/community/posts/360027982751-What-happened-to-the-knowledge-map> · confidence: high · version-sensitive

### 8. Khan Academy's current mastery display is a close precedent for our states: five ordered levels per skill, a headline percentage that counts only the top two, levels that can drop, and a post-activity summary of what moved.

Help Center: levels are Not started, Attempted, Familiar, Proficient, Mastered; 'Your mastery percentage shows the percentage of skills in the course that are currently at the Proficient or Mastered level'; 'Your skill level can move up or down'; 'After completing an activity, you'll see a summary showing which skills leveled up, leveled down, or remained at the same level'. Mastered requires already being Proficient and then answering correctly on a later mixed-skill assessment (support article 5548760867853), which parallels our 'learned needs a correct delayed answer'.

Source: <https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/115002552631-What-are-Course-and-Unit-Mastery> · confidence: high · version-sensitive

### 9. Duolingo replaced its skill tree with a single guided path to cut confusion about what to do next and to build spaced review into the sequence; the cost was a backlash over lost choice.

Duolingo blog (2022): 'We often hear from learners that they're not sure whether they're using Duolingo the correct or best way'; 'We've also built practice sessions into your path to ensure you're regularly revisiting material'. NBC News, 25 Aug 2022, quoting Luis von Ahn: 'The two goals that we had were decreasing confusion and increasing learning outcomes'; users complained about losing the ability to choose easier lessons. For us the feed already plays the role of the guided path, so the map is a reflection and entry-point screen, not the main navigation.

Source: <https://blog.duolingo.com/new-duolingo-home-screen-design> · confidence: high · version-sensitive

### 10. Math Academy, the product most built around a knowledge graph, shows students a course graph that fills in, but its own lead says the visual is under-used; the graph does its work in task selection.

Justin Skycak (CS Primer Show 23): 'Currently, we show a knowledge graph of the course. You can see, maybe there are 300 topics in the course, and as you complete topics, they fill in.' 'One thing we want to improve in the future is putting more leverage behind our knowledge graph visualization', including a time lapse and showing 'your knowledge decaying, peeling back, or fading'. These are stated plans, not shipped or evaluated features.

Source: <https://www.justinmath.com/cs-primer-show-23/> · confidence: high · version-sensitive

### 11. Execute Program uses almost our state model (Locked, Unlocked, Active, Learned) and shows only the share of lessons Learned; the known criticism is that a display that tops out at 'Learned' makes users feel finished.

Andy Matuschak's note: Locked is 'when one or more prerequisites is not Learned', Active is 'when you've worked through a lesson but haven't reviewed it', Learned is 'when you've reviewed a lesson at least once'; 'The presentation tops out at Learned, which is achieved when the reader has reviewed a lesson only once'; 'when users have reviewed all lessons in a course once, they broadly feel like they're done'. Execute Program's own page confirms prerequisite-aware ordering and four review levels at 2 days, 1 week, 3 weeks, 2 months (executeprogram.com/spaced-repetition).

Source: <https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zHJBriKPXFC2VtF8MVCdj7K> · confidence: high · version-sensitive

### 12. The 'pretty but not useful' criticism of graph views is aimed at global, untyped graphs you cannot act on; users report the local graph around one note is the useful part.

Obsidian forum thread (Nov 2023): 'The graph view turns out to be nothing than a bunch of dots which represent notes and tags, but you can't actually do anything with it'; 'fiddling with criteria to get what I wanted was a complete waste of time'; local graph described as more intuitive, global graph used mainly to spot orphan notes. This is anecdotal forum evidence, and those vaults have hundreds of untyped nodes; our graph has 40 nodes, typed edges and an action per node, so the criticism transfers only in part.

Source: <https://forum.obsidian.md/t/whats-the-point-of-the-graph-view-how-are-you-using-it/71316> · confidence: medium

### 13. The other shipped progress displays are not graphs, and 'heatmap' means two different things: calendar heatmaps and rings encode activity or daily goals, not per-concept mastery.

AoPS Alcumus: one bar per topic that 'will turn green when ... you've demonstrated enough of an understanding of the topic to pass. It will turn blue if you go above and beyond passing to achieve mastery'. Anki manual (docs.ankiweb.net/stats.html): the calendar graph 'shows past card review activity', while mastery is a separate card-count chart (new, young, mature at 21+ days). Apple HIG (activity-rings): rings 'show an individual's daily progress toward Move, Exercise, and Stand goals', 'Never change the colors of the rings', 'Always display Activity rings on a black background'. Brilliant's help page describes 'guided sequences of courses'. What the brief calls a heatmap is a mastery matrix in the Mastery Grids sense.

Source: <https://artofproblemsolving.com/school/handbook/current/alcumus> · confidence: high · version-sensitive

### 14. Evidence for skill trees as a learning progress display is thin: one small pilot, positive on motivation, with the main lesson that every node needs a clear description.

Tondello and Nacke (GamiLearn 2019), 16 students in one university course: overall experience very positive or positive for 11 of 16; 12 of 15 preferred the skill tree to numerical grades; a participant said they 'enjoyed seeing my progress', another found the mapping between tasks and skills unclear. First lesson learned: 'The tree must provide clear descriptions of each skill and what students are expected to accomplish'. No comparison with other displays and no learning measure. No primary research on game skill-tree comprehension was found.

Source: <https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-2497/paper15.pdf> · confidence: medium

## Open questions

- No study found compares a mastery grid with a prerequisite graph on actual learning or actual next-step choices; the one direct comparison (Bull et al. 2016) is stated preference from screenshots, 33 university students, desktop. Whether its ranking holds for working developers on a phone is unknown.
- Should 'seen' show as partial fill? It makes the map move early in the demo, and the endowed progress effect (Nunes and Dreze 2006: 34% vs 19% loyalty-card completion) suggests visible early progress motivates, but I could only open a secondary summary of that paper, it is a consumer study, and partial fill may read as partial mastery, which contradicts 'mastery moves only from retrieval'.
- Do the cross-topic RELATED_TO edges (18 of 22) need to be visible on the map at all, or is the 'why this reel' chip in the feed enough to tell the 'follows your curiosity across a graph' story? No evidence either way.
- How prominent should the due marker be? Duolingo once showed decaying skill strength and later hid it; I could not open a primary Duolingo source explaining why, so the risk that due markers feel like nagging is unverified. With the demo clock (reviews due after 60-120 s) markers may also appear and clear too fast to read.
- A five-step ramp plus amber and lock overlays on the existing dark gradient (#0B1220 to #13213D) has not been checked for contrast or colour-blind safety; that needs a test on a real phone and is outside what I researched.
- Not settled from primary sources: the full text of Ghoniem et al. 2004 (the 'above twenty vertices' claim comes from its abstract as quoted in search results); Kumon's and Roam's progress or graph views (nothing primary opened); what Brilliant's progress visual actually encodes (its help page only describes guided course sequences).
